## Overview

Session 3 marked a transition from exploring the purposes of modular peer review to designing questions, conditions, and structures required to support review across different modular research objects.

The session opened with participants sharing the most valuable feedback they had received.

**Key highlights:**

- Actionable, constructive comments tailored to goals
- Feedback that built confidence and clarified next steps
- Insightful reviews that connected past progress, current challenges, and future possibilities

**Discussion then shifted to what makes reviews effective:**

- Context is essential: access to data, evidence, code, and author goals
- Modularity helps: reviewers are better when focusing on specific, connected project parts
- Strong reviewers know their limits and aim to advance knowledge, not defend opinions

Barriers to review included missing data, unclear instructions, dense writing, broad requests, inconsistent standards, bias, and the pressure to be the perfect expert. The recurring challenge of large, lengthy, complex review tasks highlighted the appeal of modular review: breaking big projects into manageable, focused parts.

**Main takeaway:** Reviewers excel with clear goals, context, and evidence. Most barriers come from process design, not reviewers themselves.

Participants then moved into breakout groups to begin developing review questions and identifying the conditions required to support successful review. Three breakout groups formed during the session: Preregistration, Data, and Figures & Visualizations.

## Breakout Group: Preregistration

The preregistration group began by discussing the distinction between preregistration and registered reports, noting that while the two share similarities, they serve different purposes and may require different review approaches. The group focused on identifying questions that could be applied broadly across disciplines before considering a more specialized methodological review.

### Key Review Questions

Participants identified several foundational questions:

- Is the rationale for the study clearly described?
- Are the research questions, objectives, or hypotheses clearly stated?
- Is the study design appropriate to answer the research question?
- Is the proposed sample size appropriate and justified?
- Is the study ethically sound and feasible?
- Will the study contribute valuable knowledge regardless of whether results are positive, negative, or null?
- Are plans for missing data, exclusions, and analytical decisions adequately described?

Participants noted that preregistration review differs from many traditional review activities because it evaluates a future plan rather than completed work. Reviewers must assess whether the proposed design is likely to generate meaningful and trustworthy results while allowing sufficient flexibility to accommodate the realities of research.

### Conditions for Successful Review

Participants identified several requirements for effective preregistration review:

- Clear reviewer guidance and expectations
- Information about the intended purpose and scope of the review
- Access to supporting materials and protocols
- Clarity regarding what constitutes a successful preregistration
- Support for evaluating elements outside a reviewer's expertise
- Mechanisms that allow reviews to remain useful and transferable to later stages of the research lifecycle

A key challenge identified is balancing rigor with flexibility. Reviewers must spot the difference between valid methodological changes and practices that undermine preregistration.

## Breakout Group: Data

The data group explored the challenges of reviewing datasets as standalone research objects.

An early observation was that "data" encompasses a wide range of materials, including raw data, processed data, metadata, images, and derived datasets. Participants emphasized that review requirements may differ substantially depending on the type of data under consideration.

### Key Review Questions

The group generated questions focused on understanding, interpreting, and reusing data:

- Is the purpose of the dataset clearly described?
- Is this raw, processed, or published data?
- Is sufficient metadata available to understand and interpret the data?
- Is the dataset complete?
- Are collection methods and assumptions documented?
- Are uncertainty, accuracy, and precision adequately described?
- Are open and reusable file formats used?
- Can the dataset be understood without additional undocumented materials?
- Are there inconsistencies or quality concerns within the dataset?

Participants also discussed reuse and interoperability, including the relationship between datasets and associated workflows, analysis pipelines, and computational environments.

### Conditions for Successful Review

Several enabling conditions emerged:

- Comprehensive metadata and documentation
- Access to associated workflows and analytical context
- Clear descriptions of collection and processing methods
- Guidance for reviewers when questions do not apply to a particular dataset
- Transparency regarding assumptions and limitations

Participants discussed the FAIR and CARE principles, suggesting they should be reviewed separately rather than as a single category.

Reviewers agreed that datasets need context—how the data was generated, processed, and intended for use. It was critical for the review of data to enable future reuse.

## Breakout Group: Figures & Visualizations

The figures and visualizations group focused on both the quality of the visual artifact itself and the integrity of the process used to create it. Participants noted that visualizations occupy a unique position in research communication. They are both analytical outputs and communication tools, making review more complex than evaluating either data or narrative alone.

### Key Review Questions

The group identified questions relating to accessibility, communication, integrity, and reproducibility:

- Is the purpose of the visualization clear?
- Is the figure understandable without extensive reference to the accompanying text?
- Are labels, legends, scales, and annotations sufficient?
- Is the visualization accessible to low-vision and colorblind readers?
- Are uncertainty and confidence appropriately represented?
- Does the figure faithfully represent the underlying data?
- Is the figure reproducible?
- Are data sources, code, software, and transformations documented?
- Has AI been used in the creation of the figure, and if so, how?

Participants also explored Cairo's visualization principles, highlighting five qualities that may be useful review lenses:

- Truthful
- Functional
- Beautiful
- Insightful
- Enlightening

Discussion focused particularly on truthfulness and functionality as core dimensions of review.

### Conditions for Successful Review

Participants identified several requirements:

- Access to underlying data
- Access to software, code, and production methods
- Context regarding the intended message of the visualization
- Information about transformations, filters, and processing steps
- Clarity regarding what integrity checks have already been completed
- Appropriate reviewer expertise

The group also discussed the growing role of AI-generated figures and code, raising questions about transparency, attribution, and research integrity.

## Cross-Group Observations

Several themes emerged consistently across all breakout groups.

### 1. Purpose Must Be Clear

Participants repeatedly emphasized that reviewers need to understand what an object is intended to accomplish before they can evaluate it effectively.

1. For preregistration, this meant understanding the research question and study objectives.
2. For data, this meant understanding intended use and reuse.
3. For figures, this meant understanding the message the visualization is intended to communicate.

Without a clear understanding of purpose, review becomes inconsistent and subjective.

### 2. Context Is Essential

Across all groups, participants emphasized that research objects cannot be reviewed in isolation. Reviewers require context in the form of:

- Metadata
- Documentation
- Methodological rationale
- Data provenance
- Workflow information
- Intended use cases

Context was consistently viewed as a prerequisite for meaningful review rather than supplementary information.

### 3. Reproducibility, Reuse and Traceability Matter

Participants stressed that reviewers may need to evaluate how a research object was created and its relationship to other objects. They sought:

- Clear documentation of inputs and methods
- Transparent records of data transformations and analysis steps
- Access to supporting materials

The consensus: If others can trace and understand the full workflow, the object is more reliable, credible, and reusable. The ability to trace an object from creation to output was viewed as an important signal of quality.

### 4. Review Quality Depends on Conditions, Not Reviewers

Participants emphasized that review quality depends not only on expertise but also on access to data, software, documentation, and time. Importantly, reviews can only address what is available at the time of review—science is always provisional, and assessments reflect a snapshot of the work as it stands. Even skilled reviewers may struggle if critical information is missing. Modular review systems must recognize these limits and ensure reviewers are supported with the context and resources needed for fair assessments.

### 5. Building on Existing Review Practices

A significant theme across discussions was the recognition that many review questions already exist.

Participants repeatedly referenced existing review frameworks, reporting guidelines, repository standards, FAIR and CARE principles, journal review forms, and community-developed evaluation practices.

A key theme: Rather than reinventing the wheel, modular peer review should build on established frameworks, standards, and guidelines. The focus should be on:

1. Identifying widely used review questions
2. Adapting them for modular contexts or research objects
3. Spotting gaps where new questions are truly needed

This approach reflects the modular principle: reuse and adapt existing components instead of starting from scratch.

### 6. Tension Between Standardization and Flexibility

Participants noted a tension between universal review approaches and those tailored to specific research objects. While some review questions can be applied broadly, others are discipline- or method-specific. This points to the need for modular review systems that combine a shared core structure with specialized extensions for different research objects.

## Emerging Questions

Several questions remained unresolved and will need future refinement:

- Which review questions are universal across research objects?
- Which review questions are object-specific?
- What information must accompany an object to make review possible?
- How should reviews be structured and represented?
- How can review outputs remain useful beyond a single review event?
- How can multiple reviewers contribute complementary expertise without creating unnecessary duplication?
