## Overview

Session 4 focused on testing an [emerging system map for modular peer review](https://drive.google.com/file/d/18ifYoraD8EO1ww10Y1BLUDvAqVHHZ1TM/view). Building on the previous sessions, the working group moved from identifying reviewable objects, review purposes, and review questions to exploring how modular reviews might produce outcomes and signals that can accompany research objects over time.

Participants were invited to treat the system map as a draft framework rather than a finished model. The goal was to test whether the group could clearly define what decision is made through review, understood as the outcome, and what others can trust from that review, understood as the signal.

The session centred on three questions:

1. What works in the system map, and where does it break?
2. How should outcomes change across different objects and review purposes?
3. How should signals vary across different objects and review purposes?

## Framing: Returning to the Building Code Case Study

The session began by returning to the [building code case study](https://articles.continuousfoundation.org/articles/building-code-review) introduced earlier in the working group. This case study helped ground the idea of modular review in a familiar system in which trust is built through multiple checks throughout the lifecycle.

Three ideas from the building code example were emphasized:

**Multiple checks:** Trust is created through a series of reviews rather than a single final gate.

**Continuum-based outcomes:** Outcomes are not simply pass/fail. They can reflect nuance, conditions, timing, and scope.

**Trust that travels and accumulates:** Each review adds to a growing signal record that others can rely on later.

We acknowledged that in construction, review outcomes are important because each building is unique and objects are rarely reused. In science, however, datasets, code, and other research objects often move between projects, making it crucial that trust signals attached to them are clear and transferable across different contexts.

## The Draft System Map

Participants were then introduced to the draft modular peer review system map. The map was presented as a conceptual framework for object-based review, structured around several layers:

**Object:** What is being reviewed

**Function:** Why the review is happening

**Criteria:** The structured questions or standards used in the review

**Outcome:** The recorded result of the review at a point in time

**Signal:** The trust signals produced by the review

**Capture:** The record of attribution, contribution, and review history

The system map attempted to show how a modular review might move from a specific object, through a defined review purpose and criteria, toward an outcome and signal that can be captured and carried forward.

The session focused especially on the distinction between outcomes and signals.

An **outcome** was framed as the recorded result of a review process: where does this object stand right now?

A **signal** was framed as what the review produces for others to rely on. Examples included rigor, safety, reproducibility, compliance, fidelity, decision, clarity, and maturity.

Participants were asked to test whether this distinction held up across different research objects and review contexts.

## Breakout Exercise

Participants moved into breakout groups and worked directly on the Miro board. They were encouraged to make the board messy, add sticky notes, identify gaps, challenge labels, and note where the system map became confusing or incomplete.

The exercise asked each group to:

- Provide feedback on the overall system map
- test the outcome layer
- test the signal layer
- identify points of confusion, gaps, overlaps, or changes

The discussion generated rich feedback across all three groups.

### Breakout Summary: Figures and Visualizations

The figures and visualizations group found the system map helpful but saw opportunities to add nuance. They observed that reviewing figures often requires a range of expertise: some questions are best answered by domain experts, while others, such as accessibility or licensing, may be handled by specialists or automated tools. The group raised a key issue about the order of evaluation: signals like accessibility checks or licensing might be generated independently and ahead of full review outcomes, yet still provide meaningful trust cues. This suggests modular review should accommodate both specialist checks and broader assessments, ensuring that important signals are captured and can travel with research objects, even when they don't constitute a full review outcome.

### Breakout Summary: Data

The data group focused on the value of signals and how to capture them without losing nuance. They cautioned against collapsing rich review signals into simple metrics like scores or citations. Instead, they envisioned a trust profile for each object, where multiple qualities could be assessed and visualized separately, similar to a tasting wheel with distinct dimensions. This approach avoids reducing trust to a single number and encourages multidimensional assessment. The group emphasized the need for careful design, since signal strength can easily be oversimplified if not preserved thoughtfully. They also discussed how capture systems must ensure signals remain clear, contextual, and useful as they travel with research objects, rather than being flattened or stripped of meaning.

### Breakout Summary: Preregistration

The preregistration group explored the challenge of defining the object of review, noting that a preregistration often contains multiple components, such as study design, analysis plans, and ethics considerations. They suggested that modular review may need to assess preregistration both as a whole and in terms of its parts, each with separate criteria and outcomes. The group also highlighted confusion between outcomes and signals, emphasizing the need for clearer definitions. For preregistration, signals might include the plan's limitations, risks, novelty, or feasibility. Rather than a binary decision, the group encouraged a continuum of feedback to capture strengths, risks, and uncertainties—helping others understand the study plan in full context.

## Cross-Group Observations

Several important themes emerged across the breakout discussions. Participants generally found the system map useful for structuring modular peer review, but several groups questioned the order of the layers.

### 1. Outcomes and Signals Need Clearer Definitions

Across groups, participants struggled to distinguish outcomes from signals. This confusion was productive because it revealed where the framework needs more precision.

An outcome may be a point-in-time assessment, label, or decision. A signal may be what others can trust or interpret from that assessment. However, the boundary between the two may vary depending on the object, review purpose, and venue.

### 2. Modular Objects May Contain Smaller Objects

It raised a key insight: some objects we've named are composite. This made us question whether preregistration of a modular object is warranted in itself, given that it includes a study design, which is a summary document of statistical plans, ethics, and feasibility considerations. A figure may contain data, code, methods, design choices, and accessibility considerations.

This raises the question of whether modular review may need to support nested review, where one object can be reviewed as a whole while its components also carry their own signals.

### 3. Signals Should Preserve Richness, Not Become Another Score

The data group raised significant concerns about signal aggregation. If signals are flattened into scores, modular review could unintentionally reproduce the same problems found in existing research metrics.

Participants suggested that trust profiles, signal patterns, or multidimensional representations may better preserve nuance than a single number.

Spider maps were suggested.

### 4. Outcomes Should Move Beyond Binary Decisions

Across groups, participants pushed against binary review outcomes. They emphasized the need for outcomes that reflect continuum, context, risk, limitation, uncertainty, and stage of work.

This was especially important for preregistration, where a review may identify that a study is feasible but risky, novel but uncertain, or clear in some areas but underdeveloped in others.

## Emerging Questions

Session 4 surfaced several questions for future work:

1. Do we need outcomes if these objects are preliminary?
2. Can a research object contain smaller reviewable objects?
3. How can signals travel with objects without becoming simplistic metrics?
4. How can modular review support nuance without becoming too complex to use?
